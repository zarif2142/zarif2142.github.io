Talking to a $20 ring: reverse-engineering an undocumented Bluetooth protocol

Checksummed packets, BCD timestamps, and testing against real bytes from a $20 ring.

I bought a smart ring for about $20 on AliExpress. It was a COLMI R06, and the hardware was good for the price. The app that came with it was the problem.

The official app wanted an account, and it sent my health data to the company. I did not want either of those things. I wanted the numbers from a ring I owned, on a phone I owned, with nothing in between. So I decided to build my own iPhone app for it. That decision was easy. The next part was not, because the ring had no documentation at all.

A smart ring talks to a phone over Bluetooth Low Energy. The protocol is the set of messages the two sides exchange: which bytes mean steps, which mean heart rate, which mean a timestamp. For a documented device, that list is published. For this ring, it was not. Nobody had written it down, because the vendor had never published it. My best reference was the open-source Gadgetbridge project, which had worked out parts of the protocol for other devices. It was a starting point, not an answer.

I started by listening. The app connects to the ring, asks for data, and the ring answers in binary packets. A packet is a short string of bytes with a shape: a header, a body, and a checksum at the end. The checksum is a small number computed from the rest of the packet, so the receiver can tell whether the bytes arrived intact. If the checksum does not match, the packet is wrong, and you throw it away.

Two details took the longest. The first was timestamps. The ring stores them in BCD, which is a way of packing decimal digits into bytes, so a date that looks obvious in decimal looks strange in hex until you know what you are looking at. The second was multi-packet transfers. A night of sleep does not fit in one packet, so the ring sends it in pieces, and the app has to put the pieces back together in the right order. Miss one piece and the night is wrong. Wait too long and the ring gives up.

The rule that saved me was to test against the actual device early and often. Code that looked right on paper kept breaking the moment real bytes came off the ring. I would write a parser, run it against a real sync, watch it fail, and read the bytes again. Each failure narrowed the shape a little. Slowly, the packets started to make sense, and I pulled the parsing into RingKit, a standalone Swift package, so the protocol code could be tested on its own without the app around it.

The tests are the part I am proudest of. RingKit now has 146 tests, with 74 self-check tests and 28 app tests on top. Each one pins down a piece of behaviour I learned the hard way: a transfer that must wait for completion, a sync button that must not fight itself, a delete that must run after the filter. Every one of those started as a real failure on my own ring.

The strangest result was about the hardware, not my code. Live heart rate and SpO2 only lock on about half the time. At first I assumed my Bluetooth code was at fault. I tested the same thing with a reference client, and it failed the same way. That proved the limit was in the ring itself, not in my app. It was a relief, and a useful lesson: when a measurement fails the same way on two different clients, stop blaming your code and start blaming the sensor.

The app itself is SwiftUI, with an async/await CoreBluetooth layer that reconnects on its own and syncs in the background. It stores 7 health metrics in SwiftData, steps, heart rate, SpO2 and stress among them, and charts them by day, week and month with Swift Charts. There is a home-screen widget, health alerts, resting heart rate and export to Apple Health. None of it touches a server. The data lives on my phone.

It works on my ring every day. The next step is a full overnight sleep capture to tune the sync timeouts, which are still placeholders until they are measured against a real night. The ring cost $20. The lesson cost a few months of evenings, and it was worth it: if you want to understand a device, do not read about its protocol. Ask it for data, watch what comes back, and believe the bytes.
