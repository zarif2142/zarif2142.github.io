P&G, Data Engineering Intern

Eight pipelines that bring in 2,000+ point-of-sale files a day, and a Django app that replaced a prototype for 70+ account executives.

The pipelines

The work started with a manual job. Point-of-sale files arrived every day, and someone had to upload them by hand. It was slow, it was easy to get wrong, and it did not scale. I built 8 pipelines in Python, Azure Data Factory and Databricks that bring in 2,000+ files a day and replace those manual uploads. The saving is about 4,380 hours a year, which is the number that mattered most in the room. Hours are the one unit everyone understands.

The pipelines had to be boring in the right way. A file that arrives late, a file with a bad row, a file that arrives twice: each one needed a defined answer, not a person checking a folder. I spent as much time on the failure paths as on the happy path, because a pipeline that silently drops a file is worse than one that fails loudly.

The Django app

The second piece started as a KNIME prototype for checking retail deal sheets. It worked, but it was not something 70+ account executives could use every day. I turned it into a Django app. The job was to keep what the prototype got right, the checks themselves, and rebuild everything around them: logins, a clear view of what passed and what failed, and a flow an account executive could finish without a manual.

It is still in use by those account executives. That is the part I am proudest of. A prototype proves an idea. An app that people open every day proves the idea survived contact with real work.

What I took from it

Data engineering is mostly about trust. Nobody opens a dashboard and asks how clever the pipeline is. They ask whether the numbers are right, and whether they will still be right tomorrow. The work that earns that trust is unglamorous: checks, retries, clear errors, and a record of what ran. I learned to build that first and optimize second.
